# Static Analysis and Verification in Modern Software Supply Chains

## Software Supply Chain Vulnerabilities and Attack Vectors

As software products diversify and dependency tracking becomes harder, supply chains have grown substantially more complex and vulnerable (Soeiro, n.d.; Okafor et al., 2024). Attacks in this domain often unfold in distinct stages. Okafor et al. (2024) frame a secure supply chain around three core properties: transparency, validity, and separation. Systematizing these stages helps defenders place controls where they can disrupt attack progression before compromised artifacts reach end users. Recent incidents like the XZ utility compromise and the 3CX double-supply-chain intrusion highlight how threats propagate across interconnected components instead of through single isolated flaws (Soeiro, n.d.). As Baird and Moin (2026) note, modern attacks take advantage of cascading interactions across several vulnerable components at once. In IoT environments, widespread embedded hardware and third-party firmware further expand the attack surface, making uniform security enforcement difficult to sustain (Syed, 2024).

## Static Analysis and Dependency Attestation Standards

Analyzing source code without execution through Static Application Security Testing (SAST) remains a primary baseline for software security. Volkova (2024) reviews automated analysis methods, tracking a shift away from isolated static analyzers toward integrated pipelines that combine complementary tools. Pairing SAST with software composition analysis and dynamic testing helps cover blind spots inherent to individual scanners. Even so, automated static tools tend to generate heavy alert volumes; false positives and imprecise reports slow triage and frustrate developers, meaning static checks work best as one layer within a wider verification pipeline.

Alongside static code inspection, dependency tracking provides visibility into third-party risks. Software Bills of Materials (SBOMs) list components, version metadata, and licensing to help teams spot vulnerable dependencies (Kishimoto et al., 2025). To standardize how organizations exchange this data, Lear and Rose (2023) introduce a YANG data model for reporting SBOM and exposure information. Baird and Moin (2026) build on standard SBOM formats by structuring vulnerability-enriched CycloneDX files into heterogeneous graphs, connecting components to known CVEs via typed relational edges. Generating reliable SBOMs at scale remains difficult in practice; Kishimoto et al. (2025) show that automated outputs routinely require manual intervention to conform with profiles like SPDX Lite. Beyond component inventories, supply chain frameworks tie transparency into validity and separation guarantees (Okafor et al., 2024), alongside build attestation mechanisms designed to verify hermetic, reproducible build pipelines.

## Critical Limitations of Pre-Deployment Verification

Despite their utility, pre-deployment verification mechanisms carry distinct structural blind spots. SBOMs are fundamentally retrospective catalogs of known CVEs (Kishimoto et al., 2025; Baird & Moin, 2026). They offer no visibility into zero-day flaws, nor can a complete component manifest predict how software will behave against malicious runtime inputs. Soeiro (n.d.) observes that standard composition tools, dependency trees, and attack graphs fail to capture the complex, security-relevant interactions that characterize modern multi-stage supply chain attacks. Similarly, Baird and Moin (2026) point out that evaluating security findings on an isolated, per-CVE basis overlooks the chained vulnerabilities that drive major intrusions.

Static analyzers run into comparable boundaries. Operating without runtime execution context, SAST tools frequently miss vulnerabilities tied to environmental variables, misconfigurations, or dynamic data flows, while excessive alert rates cause triage fatigue. The practical quality of SBOM generation introduces further risk: Kishimoto et al. (2025) found widespread errors in automatically generated manifests, which needed both automated and human remediation to meet SPDX Lite specifications. Soeiro (n.d.) also notes that most production SBOMs cannot be ingested out of the box, with existing validation tools frequently contradicting one another.

## Synthesis and Future Directions

Because no single pre-deployment mechanism covers the entire threat landscape, supply chain defense requires layered verification. Static checks, manifest transparency, and build attestations each target different properties, but none fully mitigate runtime attacks or multi-stage exploit chains (Okafor et al., 2024; Soeiro, n.d.; Baird & Moin, 2026).

Improving manifest generation accuracy and ingestion tooling is an immediate priority, given how frequently raw SBOMs require post-processing (Kishimoto et al., 2025; Soeiro, n.d.). Beyond flat CVE lists, graph-learning techniques that model attack paths directly from dependency topology offer a promising approach for detecting cascaded threats (Baird & Moin, 2026). Large language models have also been proposed for interpreting dense SBOM structures, though their practical reliability requires thorough empirical validation (Iyenghar, 2026). For now, static analysis and SBOMs serve as foundational yet partial components of supply chain risk management; bridging the remaining gaps will require stronger tool precision alongside empirical studies mapping verification controls to observed breach stages.
